6 AI Prompts Copy & Run IT Professionals No Setup Required

These are the six situations where your work becomes invisible — not because you didn't do it, but because no one in the room knows how to narrate it. Each prompt gives Claude enough context to write it the way a leader would. You fill in the brackets. Claude does the framing.

How to use these

  1. 1 Open Claude (claude.ai or Claude Code). Any version works — these don't require advanced models.
  2. 2 Click a template in the sidebar. Read the scenario — if it matches your situation, click Copy Prompt.
  3. 3 Paste into Claude. Replace every [BRACKET] with your real information. The more specific you are, the better the output.
  4. 4 Review, edit, send. Claude gives you the structure and framing. You own the final words. Never send AI output without reading it.

Template 01

Executive Status Update

The situation: You need to send a weekly or monthly status update to leadership. Most people send a list of tasks completed. This prompt produces a narrative that connects your work to what the organization actually cares about — so you're not just reporting, you're being seen.

Prompt — copy and fill in the brackets
You are helping me write a professional status update for my leadership team.

Context about me:
- My role: [YOUR TITLE — e.g., "Senior Infrastructure Manager"]
- My team or domain: [YOUR TEAM OR AREA — e.g., "Network and Security Operations, 6 people"]
- Who receives this: [AUDIENCE — e.g., "my VP and the other Directors in the monthly leadership sync"]

What I worked on this period (paste your raw notes):
[YOUR LIST — bullet points, rough notes, whatever you have. Don't clean it up — just paste it.]

What leadership is focused on right now:
[1-2 sentences — e.g., "Q3 priority is reducing vendor spend and completing the ERP migration on time."]

Write a status update that:
1. Opens with the business outcome, not the task — what moved forward, not what I did
2. Connects my work to the organizational priority above
3. Surfaces one upcoming item leadership should be aware of or may need to weigh in on
4. Stays under 150 words
5. Reads as strategic, not operational — like a Director briefing peers, not a PM reporting to a boss

Format: 3 short paragraphs. No bullet lists. No jargon or acronyms unless they're universal in our org.
Customization notes
  • The "organizational priority" field is the most important one. If you don't know what leadership is focused on, check the last all-hands deck or your manager's most recent update.
  • Paste your raw notes without editing them first. Claude will extract the signal — you don't need to pre-organize.
  • If the output sounds too formal, add: "Make the tone more like a trusted colleague briefing a peer, not a subordinate reporting up."
What you get

A 3-paragraph status update (~130 words) that leads with business impact, connects directly to a stated org priority, and closes with a forward-looking note. Leadership reads it in 45 seconds and understands what you're doing and why it matters.

Template 02

Performance Review Narrative

The situation: It's review season. Most people submit a bullet list of accomplishments and hope the manager fills in the framing. This prompt writes your self-assessment the way someone one level above you would write it — focused on impact and organizational contribution, not task completion.

Prompt — copy and fill in the brackets
You are helping me write my performance review self-assessment.

Context:
- My role: [YOUR TITLE]
- Time in this role: [X years / X months]
- My manager's level: [e.g., "Director" or "VP"]
- Our review framework focuses on: [WHAT YOUR COMPANY EVALUATES — e.g., "impact, collaboration, and growth mindset" or "results, leadership behaviors, and development"]

My accomplishments this year (raw list — don't clean it up):
[PASTE YOUR LIST — specifics, numbers, projects, decisions, anything you remember. More is better here.]

What the organization was focused on this year:
[1-2 sentences — e.g., "We migrated 3 legacy systems, reduced vendor spend by 18%, and expanded the team from 4 to 9."]

Write a self-assessment narrative that:
1. Opens with the macro contribution — what the organization can do now that it couldn't do before, because of my work
2. Shows evidence of impact, not just activity — replaces "I did X" with "X resulted in Y"
3. Includes at least one moment that demonstrates leadership behavior above my current level
4. Positions my goals for next year in terms of organizational need, not personal ambition
5. Reads as if written by someone who is already performing one level above their current role

Length: 300-400 words. First person. Confident and specific — not arrogant, not hedging.
Customization notes
  • The "leadership behavior above my current level" point is where most people undersell. If you made a decision that your manager usually makes, mention it. If you navigated a cross-functional conflict, mention it.
  • If your company uses a specific competency framework (e.g., "influence without authority"), add that exact phrase to the prompt so Claude targets it.
  • Run the output through your own edit pass — Claude will write confidently. Soften anything that doesn't sound like your natural voice.
What you get

A 300-400 word self-assessment written at the register of your next level up. Hits your company's stated competencies, leads with organizational impact, and sets up your next conversation about advancement without sounding like you're lobbying.

Template 03

Project Positioning Memo

The situation: You're leading or deeply involved in a high-visibility project. There will be a meeting — or a series of conversations — where leadership forms their impression of who drove it. This prompt helps you get there first, clearly, without sounding self-promotional.

Prompt — copy and fill in the brackets
You are helping me write a project update memo that clearly positions my role and surfaces key decisions for leadership.

Context:
- Project name: [PROJECT NAME]
- My specific role: [YOUR ROLE — e.g., "Technical lead. I own the architecture decisions and vendor coordination. A PM owns scheduling and stakeholder comms."]
- Team structure: [BRIEFLY — e.g., "Cross-functional team of 8. I am one of two technical resources."]
- Current stage: [WHERE YOU ARE — e.g., "UAT complete. Go-live is in 3 weeks."]
- Audience: [WHO GETS THIS — e.g., "My VP and the CTO. Neither is in the daily weeds."]

What I have specifically done on this project:
[BE SPECIFIC — decisions you made, problems you solved, blockers you removed, tradeoffs you navigated. The more concrete, the better.]

Risks or open items that leadership should be aware of:
[1-2 honest risks — e.g., "Vendor delivery is 4 days behind. If they miss the revised date, we need a go/no-go call."]

Write a project memo that:
1. Opens with current status and business impact in 2 sentences — what is true right now
2. Names my specific decisions and contributions in the body — matter-of-factly, not as a brag
3. Surfaces the risks with enough context for leadership to act or decide
4. Closes with a specific ask: approval, awareness, a decision, or a call
5. Reads like a leader informing stakeholders — not an IC asking for recognition

Format: Short paragraphs only. No bullets. Under 250 words. Send it before the meeting where this project gets discussed.
Customization notes
  • The "what I specifically did" section is the one most people leave vague. Force yourself to name actual decisions: "I chose vendor X over Y because..." or "I resolved the security blocker by..."
  • The closing ask is critical. A memo without an ask is an FYI. An FYI doesn't require anyone to think about you.
  • Send this 24-48 hours before the relevant meeting, not after. Timing is a visibility move.
What you get

A clean, under-250-word memo that names your contributions without self-promotion, surfaces the decisions leadership actually needs to make, and positions you as the person with their hands on the wheel.

Template 04

LinkedIn Visibility Post

The situation: You have something worth saying — a real lesson from work, a moment that changed how you think, a pattern you've noticed after 20 years in IT. This prompt writes it the way a credible practitioner sounds, not a content creator performing expertise.

Prompt — copy and fill in the brackets
You are helping me write a LinkedIn post about a real experience at work.

Context about me:
- My role: [YOUR TITLE]
- My domain: [e.g., "enterprise IT, 20 years. Currently Director of Digital Solutions in healthcare."]
- The audience I write for: [e.g., "technical managers and IT leads who want to move into leadership roles"]

What I want to share (rough — don't clean it up):
[DESCRIBE THE MOMENT OR LESSON IN 2-4 SENTENCES. What happened? What did you realize? What would you do differently? It can be messy — that's fine.]

Write a LinkedIn post that:
1. Opens with the exact moment — drop the reader into a specific scene, not a lesson or general observation
2. Shares one specific insight: what I learned or what I'd do differently — not a list of takeaways
3. Ends with a question or observation that invites response — something that makes someone with 15 years of experience want to reply
4. Reads like I'm talking to one specific person over coffee, not broadcasting to a feed
5. Avoids all of: "excited to share," "humbled," "game-changer," "unpopular opinion," "a thread," "let me explain," and any opener that begins with "I"

Length: 100-140 words. Line breaks between paragraphs for LinkedIn formatting. Direct and specific — no fluff.
Customization notes
  • The "drop into a scene" opener is what separates a post people read from one they scroll past. Be specific: "Last Tuesday, my VP called me into her office" lands. "I've been thinking about leadership lately" doesn't.
  • If the post ends up too long, ask Claude to trim it to 100 words. Shorter usually performs better with the IT audience.
  • Post 2-3 times before evaluating what lands. LinkedIn is slow-burn visibility — one post doesn't tell you anything.
What you get

A 100-140 word LinkedIn post that opens with a real moment, shares one specific insight from your actual experience, and ends with something worth responding to. Reads like a person, not a content machine.

Template 05

Meeting Follow-up That Builds Credit

The situation: You just walked out of a high-stakes meeting where you contributed, facilitated, or made a decision. The follow-up email most people send is a bullet list of action items. This prompt turns it into a visibility move — a record of who contributed what, written in a way that reinforces your role.

Prompt — copy and fill in the brackets
You are helping me write a meeting follow-up email.

Context:
- What the meeting was: [MEETING NAME/PURPOSE — e.g., "Q3 architecture review with the CTO and VP of Engineering"]
- My role in it: [e.g., "I presented two migration options and answered technical questions for 45 minutes"]
- Who attended: [BRIEFLY — e.g., "CTO, VP of Engineering, 3 Directors, me"]
- What happened (rough notes — your own words):
[WHAT WAS DISCUSSED, WHAT DECISIONS WERE MADE, WHAT I CONTRIBUTED — messy is fine, include everything you remember]
- Who gets this email: [RECIPIENTS — e.g., "all attendees, including the CTO"]

Write a follow-up email that:
1. Opens by capturing the decision made or the outcome agreed on — not "per our meeting today" or "as discussed"
2. Names my specific contributions in the body — factually, in a way that reads as a summary, not self-promotion
3. Lists next steps with clear owner names (not "the team") and specific dates
4. Is written at the register of a senior leader — measured, clear, accountable
5. Does NOT ask for validation of what was said or whether the notes are accurate — this email confirms, it doesn't request review

Format: Short subject line. 3-4 paragraphs, under 200 words. Professional but direct. Send within 2 hours of the meeting.
Customization notes
  • The 2-hour window matters. A same-day follow-up is a leadership behavior. A next-day follow-up is just notes.
  • Naming the owner of each next step (a real name, not "the team") is the most important structural move. Ambiguity in follow-ups is where credit disappears.
  • If the output includes a question to the group, remove it. This email does not ask — it confirms.
What you get

A clean, under-200-word follow-up that leads with the decision, names contributors accurately, and closes with owned next steps. It serves as the official record of the meeting — and you wrote it.

Template 06

Champion Brief

The situation: You have a sponsor or advocate — someone who talks about you in rooms you're not in. Most people assume their sponsor knows what to say. They don't. This prompt gives your champion a half-page tool they can actually use when your name comes up in the right conversation.

Prompt — copy and fill in the brackets
You are helping me write a "champion brief" — a short document I give to my sponsor or advocate so they can represent me accurately in conversations I'm not part of.

Context about me:
- My role: [YOUR TITLE]
- Time in organization: [X years]
- My key accomplishments (last 6-12 months):
[3-5 bullet points — your own words, as specific as possible. Include numbers where you have them.]
- What I'm aiming for: [e.g., "A Director-level role" or "to lead the platform modernization initiative next year"]
- My sponsor's relationship to me: [e.g., "My skip-level. She is in the quarterly executive talent review."]
- How I want to be perceived: [2-3 words — e.g., "strategic, technically credible, ready for more scope"]

Write a champion brief that:
1. Summarizes who I am in one sentence — role, impact, how long
2. Gives 3 specific proof points my sponsor can mention naturally in conversation — concrete, not vague
3. States clearly what opportunity I'm ready for and why the timing makes sense for the organization
4. Gives my sponsor one "door-opener" sentence they can use naturally when my name comes up
5. Reads like something my sponsor would be comfortable saying out loud — plain language, not polished corporate copy

Format: Half a page maximum. Bullet points for the proof points only. Everything else is plain paragraphs. This is a tool, not a showcase.
Customization notes
  • The "door-opener sentence" is the deliverable your sponsor will actually use. If Claude's version sounds too formal, ask: "Rewrite the door-opener sentence so it sounds like something a senior executive would say casually in a hallway conversation."
  • Give this to your sponsor in a 1:1, not via email. Walk them through it. Ask if they'd change anything. That conversation is the sponsorship activation.
  • Update it every 6 months or after a significant accomplishment. A stale champion brief is worse than none — it makes you look like you stopped growing.
What you get

A half-page champion brief with a one-sentence summary, 3 specific proof points, a clear statement of readiness, and a door-opener your sponsor can use tomorrow. The difference between a sponsor who vaguely supports you and one who specifically advocates for you is usually this document.
