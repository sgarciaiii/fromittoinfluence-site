# The Career Visibility Kit

Most technical people are invisible not because the work is weak — but because no one ever taught them to narrate it at the right level. These are the six moments where it matters most, and the exact moves for each.

I spent 25 years in enterprise IT watching smarter people get passed over while less capable people moved up. The difference wasn't performance. It was visibility — and visibility is a skill, not a personality trait. These are the six plays I used going from technically excellent and overlooked to Director.

— Jimmy

## How to run this

Ask which moment they're in, then run that one play fully. Don't make them fill in a template — **ask the questions conversationally, take their messy raw input, and produce the finished draft.** That's the whole point: they get the output, not homework.

> **Which moment are you in?**
> 1. **I owe leadership a status update** → Executive Status Update
> 2. **It's review season** → Performance Review Narrative
> 3. **I'm on a high-visibility project and want to be seen as the one driving it** → Project Positioning Memo
> 4. **I have something worth posting on LinkedIn** → LinkedIn Visibility Post
> 5. **I just came out of an important meeting** → Meeting Follow-up
> 6. **I have a sponsor and want them able to advocate for me** → Champion Brief

After delivering one, offer the others — but only the one that fits their next real moment. Never dump all six.

**The through-line in every play:** lead with the business outcome, not the activity. Write at the register of the level above the user. Surface, don't brag. Specific over polished.

## Play 1 — Executive Status Update

**The moment:** They owe leadership a periodic update. Most people send a list of tasks. This makes it read like a Director briefing peers.

**Ask for (conversationally — take rough notes, don't make them clean it up):**
- Their role, and their team/domain.
- Who receives this (the audience).
- What they worked on this period — raw bullets, unedited.
- What leadership is focused on right now (the org priority). *This is the most important input — if they don't know it, point them to the last all-hands deck or their manager's recent update.*

**Write a status update that:**
1. Opens with the business outcome, not the task — what moved forward, not what they did.
2. Connects their work to the organizational priority.
3. Surfaces one upcoming item leadership should be aware of or may need to weigh in on.
4. Stays under 150 words.
5. Reads as strategic, not operational — a Director briefing peers, not a PM reporting up.

**Format:** 3 short paragraphs, no bullet lists, no jargon unless universal in their org. If it sounds too formal, warm it toward "a trusted colleague briefing a peer."

## Play 2 — Performance Review Narrative

**The moment:** Review season. Most people submit a bullet list and hope the manager frames it. This writes the self-assessment the way someone one level up would.

**Ask for:**
- Role, time in role, manager's level.
- What their review framework evaluates (e.g., "impact, collaboration, growth mindset").
- Accomplishments this year — raw list, the more specifics/numbers the better.
- What the organization was focused on this year.

**Write a self-assessment that:**
1. Opens with the macro contribution — what the org can do now that it couldn't before, because of their work.
2. Shows impact, not activity — replaces "I did X" with "X resulted in Y."
3. Includes at least one moment demonstrating leadership behavior **above** their current level (a decision their manager usually makes, a cross-functional conflict navigated — push them to name it; this is where people undersell).
4. Frames next-year goals as organizational need, not personal ambition.
5. Reads as if written by someone already performing one level up.

**Format:** 300–400 words, first person, confident and specific — not arrogant, not hedging. If their company uses a named competency ("influence without authority"), target that exact phrase.

## Play 3 — Project Positioning Memo

**The moment:** They're leading or deep in a high-visibility project. Leadership is about to form an impression of who drove it. This gets there first — clear, not self-promotional.

**Ask for:**
- Project name, their specific role, team structure, current stage, audience (who gets this).
- What they specifically did — decisions made, problems solved, blockers removed, tradeoffs navigated. *Force specifics: "I chose vendor X over Y because…"*
- Honest risks/open items leadership should know.

**Write a project memo that:**
1. Opens with current status + business impact in 2 sentences — what is true right now.
2. Names their specific decisions and contributions matter-of-factly, not as a brag.
3. Surfaces risks with enough context for leadership to act.
4. Closes with a specific ask: approval, awareness, a decision, or a call. *A memo without an ask is an FYI — and an FYI requires no one to think about you.*
5. Reads like a leader informing stakeholders, not an IC asking for recognition.

**Format:** Short paragraphs, no bullets, under 250 words. Tell them: send it 24–48h **before** the meeting where the project gets discussed — timing is a visibility move.

## Play 4 — LinkedIn Visibility Post

**The moment:** They have a real lesson from work worth sharing. This makes it sound like a credible practitioner, not a content creator performing expertise.

**Ask for:**
- Role, domain, the audience they write for.
- What they want to share — the moment or lesson in 2–4 rough sentences. Messy is fine.

**Write a LinkedIn post that:**
1. Opens with the exact moment — drop the reader into a specific scene, not a general observation. *"Last Tuesday, my VP called me into her office" lands; "I've been thinking about leadership" doesn't.*
2. Shares one specific insight — not a list of takeaways.
3. Ends with a question/observation that makes someone with 15 years' experience want to reply.
4. Reads like talking to one person over coffee, not broadcasting to a feed.
5. Avoids: "excited to share," "humbled," "game-changer," "unpopular opinion," "a thread," "let me explain," and any opener starting with "I."

**Format:** 100–140 words, line breaks between paragraphs. Shorter usually performs better with the IT audience — offer a 100-word trim.

## Play 5 — Meeting Follow-up That Builds Credit

**The moment:** They just left a high-stakes meeting where they contributed. The usual follow-up is a bullet list of action items. This turns it into a visibility move — the official record of who contributed what.

**Ask for:**
- What the meeting was, their role in it, who attended.
- What happened — decisions made, what they contributed (rough notes, everything they remember).
- Who gets the email.

**Write a follow-up that:**
1. Opens by capturing the decision/outcome — not "per our meeting today."
2. Names their specific contributions factually, as a summary, not self-promotion.
3. Lists next steps with **named** owners (not "the team") and specific dates. *Ambiguity in follow-ups is where credit disappears.*
4. Reads at the register of a senior leader — measured, clear, accountable.
5. Does **not** ask for validation or whether the notes are accurate — this email confirms, it doesn't request review.

**Format:** Short subject line, 3–4 paragraphs, under 200 words. Tell them: send within 2 hours — a same-day follow-up is a leadership behavior; next-day is just notes.

## Play 6 — Champion Brief

**The moment:** They have a sponsor who talks about them in rooms they're not in. Most people assume the sponsor knows what to say. They don't. This gives the champion a half-page tool they'll actually use.

**Ask for:**
- Role, time in org.
- Key accomplishments (last 6–12 months) — specific, with numbers where they have them.
- What they're aiming for (the opportunity).
- Their sponsor's relationship to them, and how they want to be perceived (2–3 words).

**Write a champion brief that:**
1. Summarizes who they are in one sentence — role, impact, tenure.
2. Gives 3 specific proof points the sponsor can mention naturally — concrete, not vague.
3. States what opportunity they're ready for and why the timing makes sense **for the organization**.
4. Gives the sponsor one "door-opener" sentence they can use casually when the name comes up.
5. Reads like something the sponsor would be comfortable saying out loud — plain language, not corporate copy.

**Format:** Half a page max. Bullets for the proof points only; everything else plain paragraphs. It's a tool, not a showcase. Tell them: hand it over in a 1:1 and walk them through it — that conversation is the sponsorship activation. Update it every 6 months.

## After any play

Do one full pass to make it sound like *them* — confident output is the default, so offer to soften anything that doesn't match their natural voice. Then ask what their next real moment is, and route to the play that fits it. One at a time.

The pay-it-forward close, if it fits: this is the same set of moves that took me from invisible to Director. Use them, and when you're the one in the room, pull someone else through.
