Being technically excellent got you here. Being understood is what takes you further, and that's a skill anyone can learn.

I didn't start in management.
I started fixing what was broken.

I started as a network field tech. Then PC support. Then nine years in IT security, head down, fixing what was broken, certain that doing the work well was the job.

It wasn't the whole job. I learned that the year I crossed into executive management and saw the machinery from the other side: who gets seen, who gets written about, who's in the room when decisions get made. I'd been technically excellent and quietly overlooked for a decade, and I'd called it humility.

Then it moved fast. Service Portfolio Manager: two pay grades in one jump. Director of Operations. Director of Infrastructure. Now Director of Digital Services. Same person. Different game.

I built this to settle a debt. Someone gave me a shot while I was still in school. I tried to thank him. He told me to pay it back. Years later a mentor did it again, and called it paying it forward. I've carried both ever since. And now I carry it for my son, who's the reason I don't get to quit.

So here it is, from the inside, for every technical person who's been betting their whole career on being the best one in the room. Two people pulled me through. Now I pay it forward, to you.

It was never about working harder.

If any of this sounds familiar, it isn't a flaw in you. It's a gap nobody in IT teaches how to close, and it's very closable.

Great work doesn't speak for itself

The best technical work in the room can still go unnoticed, not because it isn't good, but because no one translated it into what leadership cares about. That's a fixable gap, not a verdict on you.

The best idea doesn't always win the room

Strong proposals get funded when they're framed in the right language, in the room that actually decides. Yours can be too, once you can see how that room works.

Leadership speaks a different dialect

Executives above the VP line filter for outcomes, not implementation. Translating your work into that dialect isn't selling out. It's a learnable skill that makes your work easy to back.

Your story is worth telling on purpose

In every org, narratives form. You get to shape yours, so the people deciding your next step actually know the full range of what you've delivered.
